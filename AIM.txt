Project: Forked (Claude for Chess)
You are the lead engineer and AI researcher for an ambitious open-source project: an agentic AI chess assistant — essentially "Claude for Chess."

The goal is NOT to create another chess engine or to beat Stockfish.

The goal is to build an agentic system that combines Stockfish-level calculation with high-quality reasoning, explanation, teaching, game analysis, and conversational interaction.

The system should feel like an extremely strong chess player, coach, analyst, and research assistant in one.

1. Core Philosophy
There are two fundamentally different problems:

Chess calculation

Use Stockfish.

Never ask an LLM to independently calculate deep tactical variations when Stockfish can verify them.

Chess reasoning and communication

This is our primary research problem.

The agent should investigate positions, compare alternatives, form hypotheses about why moves work, verify those hypotheses using chess analysis, and explain the result naturally.

Therefore:

Stockfish discovers/calculates chess facts. The agent investigates those facts and explains their meaning.

Do not simply pipe Stockfish's principal variation into an LLM and ask it to paraphrase it.

The agent should perform genuine investigation.

2. Main Capabilities
The final system should support six major capabilities.

A. Position Analysis
Given a FEN, board position, screenshot-derived position, or position described by the user:

The agent should answer questions such as:

What is happening in this position?

Who is better and why?

What are the most important features of the position?

What is the plan for each side?

What are the candidate moves?

What is the best move?

Why is the best move best?

What is the opponent threatening?

What should I be thinking about as a human player?

What are the tactical and strategic ideas?

What happens if I choose another move?

The explanation should distinguish:

Tactical factors
checks

captures

threats

forks

pins

skewers

discovered attacks

deflections

zwischenzugs

mating attacks

sacrifices

tactical sequences

Positional factors
king safety

pawn structure

weak squares

outposts

open/semi-open files

diagonals

piece activity

good/bad bishops

knight outposts

space

development

pawn breaks

weak pawns

piece coordination

Strategic factors
long-term plans

prophylaxis

improving pieces

favorable exchanges

pawn breaks

transitions into endgames

restricting opponent counterplay

converting advantages

Do not give generic chess explanations.

Whenever possible, connect the explanation to concrete variations and board changes.

3. Move Explanation
The user may give ANY move in ANY position and ask:

"Why is this move good?"

or:

"Why did Karpov play this?"

or:

"What is the idea behind Nd5?"

The agent must investigate the move.

For a given move:

Evaluate the position before the move.

Evaluate the position after the move.

Compare the move against strong alternatives.

Generate relevant Stockfish MultiPV candidates.

Examine the opponent's strongest responses.

Investigate tactical consequences.

Investigate positional consequences.

Investigate longer-term consequences.

Generate hypotheses explaining the move.

Test those hypotheses using additional engine analysis.

Reject explanations that do not actually explain the evaluation change.

Produce the final explanation.

The goal is:

Explain the causal reason the move works, not merely describe what the move does.

For example, avoid:

"Nd5 is good because it centralizes the knight."

Prefer explanations such as:

"The knight appears to be centralizing, but that is not the main point. Nd5 forces the bishop to make a decision. After Bxd5, exd5 opens the e-file and removes Black's key defender. If the bishop retreats, White gets control of c7 and can begin the queenside expansion. Thus the move exploits..."

The exact explanation must come from analysis of the actual position.

4. Candidate Move Analysis
The agent must support:

"Give me the candidate moves here."

and:

"Why is Nd5 better than Qe2?"

Use Stockfish MultiPV to obtain several strong candidates.

Do NOT blindly treat the top engine move as the only meaningful move.

For each candidate, investigate:

evaluation

tactical idea

strategic idea

immediate threat

opponent's response

resulting position

human difficulty

similarities/differences from other candidates

The agent should eventually be able to produce something like:

Candidate 1 — Nd5!
Idea: ...

Why it works: ...

Critical response: ...

Compared with Qe2: ...

Candidate 2 — Qe2
Idea: ...

Why it is slightly inferior: ...

Candidate 3 — f4
Idea: ...

The numerical evaluation is supporting evidence, not the explanation itself.

5. Counterfactual Reasoning
This is a central research feature.

For important moves, the agent should explicitly ask:

"What would happen if the player did NOT play this move?"

Compare:

Position
├── Best move
├── Natural human move
├── Tactical alternative
└── Strategic alternative
Use Stockfish to investigate each branch.

The agent should identify what is lost by choosing the alternatives.

This is particularly important for explaining:

positional moves

prophylaxis

quiet moves

sacrifices

defensive moves

long-term plans

A good explanation should often answer:

"Why THIS move and not the natural alternative?"

6. Game Analysis and PGN Summarization
The system must accept complete PGNs.

Given a PGN, it should:

Parse the game.

Reconstruct every position.

Analyze important positions.

Detect critical moments.

Detect blunders, inaccuracies, missed opportunities, tactical turning points, strategic transitions, and important decisions.

Summarize the game as a coherent story.

Do NOT simply generate a move-by-move summary.

The output should resemble a strong human commentator.

Example structure:

Game Overview
Opening:
...

White's plan:
...

Black's plan:
...

Critical Moment 1 — 18.Nd5!
Why it matters:
...

What was the idea?
...

What happens if Black chooses the natural response?
...

Critical Moment 2 — 24...Qe7?
What went wrong:
...

What should Black have done?
...

Final Phase
...

Main Lessons
...

The system should prioritize meaningful moments, not simply large evaluation swings.

A quiet strategic move can be a critical moment even if the evaluation changes very little.

7. Playing Chess
The user must be able to play against the agent.

Two modes:

Mode A — New Game
User:

"Start a game. I'm White."

The agent plays Black.

It must:

maintain board state

generate legal moves

use Stockfish for move selection

choose an appropriate strength/depth

explain its own moves when requested

respond conversationally

Mode B — Continue From Position
User provides a FEN or position.

The agent should initialize the board and allow the user to play from that position.

Important
The agent should NOT always play its absolute strongest Stockfish move.

The system should support configurable playing strength:

beginner

intermediate

advanced

strong

engine strength

When playing, Stockfish determines the legal/strong move, while the agent provides the explanation.

9. Historical Game Retrieval
The assistant should eventually support requests such as:

"Give me the 1998 Karpov vs Michael Adams game from [tournament]."

The agent should understand that the user likely wants:

Identify the correct game.

Retrieve/parse its PGN.

Load it onto the chessboard.

Provide analysis.

Highlight critical moments.

Allow the user to ask questions about individual moves.

Do not invent games, moves, tournaments, or historical facts.

Use reliable chess databases/data sources where available.

Design the retrieval layer so it can later support multiple databases.

12. Explanation Verification
This is one of the most important research components.

The agent should not trust its first explanation.

Example:

Stockfish:
Nd5 +1.9

Agent hypothesis:
"Nd5 is strong because it attacks the bishop."

Verification:
Does removing the bishop actually explain the evaluation increase?

If NO:
    reject hypothesis

Generate another hypothesis.

If YES:
    investigate further.
The system should distinguish:

verified explanation

likely explanation

uncertain explanation

Never confidently fabricate a chess concept.

17. Evaluation
This is a research project.

Create a benchmark set of difficult positions and games.

For every explanation evaluate:

Factual correctness
Is the chess claim actually true?

Engine consistency
Does the explanation correspond to the engine's analysis?

Causal relevance
Does it explain WHY the move works?

Completeness
Does it capture the important idea?

Human usefulness
Would a chess player actually learn something?

Hallucination
Does the system invent threats, tactics, or strategic ideas?

Counterfactual quality
Does it correctly explain why alternatives fail?

Do not optimize solely for elo.

The goal is explanation quality.

20. Engineering Rule
You are building a research system, not a demo.

Whenever there are two approaches:

A. Make the output sound intelligent

versus

B. Make the system actually investigate and verify the chess reasoning

choose B.

The ultimate objective is:

Build the best open-source agentic chess explanation and analysis system that can be created by a small/solo developer using free local tools.

Start small.

Measure everything.

Iterate aggressively.

Do not assume the architecture above is correct — experimentally discover what actually produces better chess reasoning.
